Unstated voters: how recent voting papers state their behavioural assumptions

[Author names and affiliations] · Draft note, November 2026

We coded 150 papers on voting from 2021–2025 (AAMAS, IJCAI, AAAI, EC, the COMSOC workshop, Social Choice and Welfare, GEB, JET, Mathematical Social Sciences). Two findings stand out:

  1. About 60 % of papers treat voters as sincere in at least one analysis without giving a reason (59 % [52–66]). Most of them do not state the assumption at all. The figure barely depends on the coder (57–62 %).
  2. About half of papers make a welfare or real-world claim that rests on an assumption they do not state (48 % [41–56]). Typically the missing assumption is that submitted ballots reflect what voters hold. This figure depends on the coder: 45 % with one coder alone, 66 % with the other.

The impression that prompted this study is therefore supported only in part. Most papers (69 % [63–76]) do say how ballots relate to preferences. Where utilities appear alongside ordinal or approval ballots, 83 % [73–92] define the link. Treating preferences, ballots and utilities as one object is thus a minority practice, concentrated in algorithmic and axiomatic papers.

Two further gaps:

  • Where one type of agent is strategic and another is not (56 % of papers with two or more acting types), fewer than half give a reason.
  • "Dichotomous preferences" are explained in terms of utility in 6 of the 12 papers that use the term.

Purely procedural papers, which need none of these assumptions, are rare (7 %). Only 1 paper in 150 says explicitly that it takes a procedural reading. All codes come from language models (Section 2).

1. Results

The codebook records what papers say, not whether their models are wrong. Each measure is computed only over the papers to which it applies. A paper with no welfare criteria and no claims beyond its results counts as not at fault on the claim measures.

Stakeholders. These figures are unweighted.

  • Voters act in 144 of the 150 papers. Their behaviour is stated in words in 93 of the 144 (65 %).
  • Voters are strategic in at least some analyses in 57 papers. What they know is stated in words in 29 of those (51 %) and not specified at all in 20 (35 %).
  • Candidates, organisers, bribers and designers act in few papers (4–11 each). When they do, their behaviour is nearly always stated.

What such claims look like

These five papers were drawn at random (seed comsoc-rationality-2026-11-17|examples, script scripts/draw_examples.py) from the 73 coded as having a claim that rests on an unstated assumption. They are the first five in that order that make claims beyond their formal results. They are paraphrased and not named, since this note names papers only as examples of good practice.

  • Algorithmic, COMSOC. Sequences of committees. The paper presents egalitarian or equitable solutions as fair to the agents, and computes satisfaction on the agents' nominations. Unstated: that the nominations reflect what the agents want.
  • Axiomatic, COMSOC. Proportional selection in a network. The paper says its methods foster a stronger connection between voters and their representatives, and that they show potential for real use. The axioms are defined on approval links. Unstated: that the links reflect voters' actual trust or preferences.
  • Distortion, AI conference. Randomised rules. The paper derives an argument for using particular rules from their distortion bounds. It states that voters' costs are consistent with their rankings, so it counts among the 69 % that state the relation. Unstated: that voters report their rankings sincerely.
  • Axiomatic, journal. Approval-based committee rules. The paper motivates its axioms by the need to choose a rule in a principled way, and to reflect voters' preferences proportionally. The axioms are defined on approval ballots. Unstated: that ballots equal preferences.
  • Welfare, journal. Who should vote. The paper concludes that it is optimal to restrict the franchise to those with the highest stakes. Welfare is computed from whether voters' opinions, which are also their votes, match the outcome. Unstated: that voters vote their opinion. In such information-aggregation models, sincere voting is not always an equilibrium (Austen-Smith and Banks, 1996).

In all five, the missing assumption is that what voters submit is what they hold.

Paper type separates the papers more sharply than venue does:

  • Papers whose object is strategic behaviour (equilibrium, strategic, empirical) almost always distinguish ballots from preferences.
  • Algorithmic and axiomatic papers account for most of the gap.

Journals publish more equilibrium papers than the AI conferences do, so part of the venue difference may reflect this mix. We did not test that.

Good practice

From the 17 papers where both coders and the adjudicator coded every relevant item as stated, chosen to cover paper types:

  • Axiomatic. Narayanan (2022) gives agents' states and their messages separate notation, and says why no one has an incentive to misreport.
  • Algorithmic. Baumeister, Selker and Wilczynski (2021) separate ballots from preferences and state what voters and the polling agency know.
  • Truthful voting justified. Mueller and Puppe (2021) distinguish reported from true peaks. They justify truthful voting by dominant strategies and evaluate outcomes on the true peaks.
  • Strategic parties, sincere voters. Debowicz, Saporiti and Wang (2021) model parties as strategic and voters as sincere, and give the reason in a footnote: with a continuum of voters, no one is pivotal.
  • Experiment. Casella, Guo and Jiang (2023) explain why the number of candidates is fixed while voters choose freely.
  • Information and interpretation. Tsakas and Xefteris (2021) state the information structure and ask for "extreme caution in the interpretation of our findings".

2. Method

Population and frame. The population is papers on voting: single- and multi-winner rules, approval committees, participatory budgeting, liquid democracy, strategic candidacy, districting, and manipulation, control and bribery. Fair division, matching and sortition are excluded. The frame is the 2021–2025 output of the nine venues, taken from proceedings listings and Crossref: 22,012 items. A keyword pre-filter and language-model screening of titles and abstracts left 710 candidates. A recall check found 0 eligible papers among 300 rejected titles.

Sample. 150 papers, stratified by venue type × year. Each venue type has a floor of 20, and the rest is allocated proportionally: AI conferences 43, COMSOC 33, EC 24, journals 50. The order within each stratum is fixed by SHA-256 hashes of a published seed. Only legal open versions were used.

To reach 150 eligible papers we coded 194, replacing papers within their stratum:

  • 39 ineligible on full text;
  • 27 with no open version (MSS 12, SCW 11, JET 2, GEB 1, EC 1);
  • 1 duplicate.

Weights are Nh · e / nh, where e is the eligibility rate observed on full text in that venue type (0.76–0.90).

Coding. The codebook has 10 item groups plus an eligibility check. It was revised once after a 10-paper pilot and then frozen. Every code carries a location and a verbatim quote of at most 12 words. 98.8 % of the 2,290 quotes in the final codes were found verbatim in the texts.

All screening, coding and adjudication was done by language models under the supervision of the author. The author wrote the research questions, approved the protocol and codebook at two checkpoints, and set the procedure. No code was changed by hand.

Pass A used Claude Opus 5.5 and pass B Claude Sonnet 5.5. Each saw only the codebook and the paper. Where A and B disagreed (614 items), Claude Opus 5.5 adjudicated: it chose A in 85 % of cases, B in 11 % and a third code in 5 %.

The measure behind finding 1, counted over all papers, was added after the results were known. Its components were pre-specified.

3. Calibration case

We coded Heitzig, Simmons and Constantino (2024), with its supplement, in the same way. It belongs to the frame but was not drawn, so it does not enter the estimates.

Stated:

  • Ballots and utilities are treated as separate objects ("such stated preferences may or may not be related in any way").
  • Sincere ballots are defined as functions of utilities.
  • Welfare is evaluated on utilities; Pareto efficiency is stated to be with respect to stated preferences.
  • Voters are modelled as sincere, lazy or strategic, with information limited to polling scores, both said explicitly.

Shortfalls:

  • The paper uses "dichotomous preferences" without saying what the two classes mean in utility terms. The term appears only when the paper refers to prior work.
  • The share of non-strategic voters is motivated by election data. The analyses with only sincere voters give no separate reason (the second coder coded no reason at all).
  • In the equilibrium analyses, the information assumptions are only implicit.
  • The coders disagreed on whether the paper's claims beyond the formal results (e.g. that "the welfare costs of fairness and consensus are small") need an unstated assumption. The adjudicator, the same model as coder A, sided with A: all needed assumptions are stated. Given the reliability of this item (κ 0.39), the question stays open.

4. Limitations

  • Language-model coders. Both coders come from one vendor, and the adjudicator is the same model as coder A. Coder B codes "not stated" more often; hence the "A only / B only" ranges. κ measures agreement between models, not validity. A human hand-check of 15 random papers is prepared but not yet included.
  • Unreliable items. Two items have κ below 0.40 and should not carry weight: whether claims need an unstated assumption (κ 0.39) and whether a stated identity comes with a reason (κ 0.25). Whether a paper refers to held preferences at all has κ 0.35 despite 93 % agreement, because 95 % of papers do (Feinstein and Cicchetti, 1990).
  • Open-access bias. 27 drawn papers had no legal open version, 23 of them in MSS and SCW.
  • Prior. The author held the impression tested here when drafting the codebook. Neutral wording, blind coders, an NA code for procedural papers and pre-specified measures reduce this influence but do not remove it.
  • "Not stated" records the text, not the authors' understanding. An assumption obvious to specialists still counts as unstated if the text does not state it.

5. A short reporting checklist

  1. Name what voters submit and what they hold. Then say how the two relate: they may differ, or they are equal, and if equal, why (strategyproofness, the setting, a procedural reading).
  2. If welfare, efficiency, proportionality or distortion is evaluated on ballots, say whether it is meant with respect to the ballots or to the preferences.
  3. For each type of acting agent, say whether it is strategic and what it knows. If one type is strategic and another is not, give the reason.
  4. If utilities appear alongside ordinal or approval ballots, define the link. If you use "dichotomous preferences", say what the two classes mean in terms of utility.
  5. For claims about real institutions or voters' well-being, name the assumptions they need, or mark them as conjecture.
  6. If the paper is purely procedural, say so. One sentence suffices; 1 paper in 150 had it.
